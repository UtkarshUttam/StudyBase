Prime factorization · Divisibility · Fundamental theorem

🌿
Factors, Multiples & Prime FactorizationCORE CONCEPT
▶

📌 Factors

  • A divides B completely → A is a factor of B
  • Factors of 12: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12
  • Number of factors of 12 = 6
  • Every number has 1 and itself as factors
  • Factors are always ≤ the number

📌 Multiples

  • B = A × k (k is integer) → B is a multiple of A
  • Multiples of 4: 4, 8, 12, 16, 20…
  • Every number is a multiple of itself
  • Multiples are always ≥ the number
  • Infinite multiples exist for any number
🌳 Prime Factorization — Factor Tree Method

HCF — Highest Common Factor

3 methods · Euclid's algorithm · Shortcuts · Solved examples

📐
HCF — Complete MethodsMOST TESTED
▶
HCF (a,b) = Greatest number dividing both a and b exactly
Also called: GCD (Greatest Common Divisor) · GCF
Method 1: Prime Factorization
STEP 1

Prime factorize each number

72 = 2³ × 3² | 108 = 2² × 3³
STEP 2

Take LOWEST power of COMMON prime factors

HCF = 2² × 3² = 4 × 9 = 36

⚡ Shortcut Rule

HCF = Product of LOWEST powers of ALL common prime factors.
LCM = Product of HIGHEST powers of ALL prime factors (common + uncommon).

Method 2: Long Division Method

📋 Steps

  1. 1
    Divide larger by smaller → get remainder
  2. 2
    Divide previous divisor by remainder
  3. 3
    Repeat until remainder = 0
  4. 4
    Last divisor = HCF

Example: HCF(84, 56)

84 ÷ 56 = 1, R = 28
56 ÷ 28 = 2, R = 0
∴ HCF(84, 56) = 28
Method 3: Euclid's Algorithm
HCF(a, b) = HCF(b, a mod b)
Repeat until remainder = 0. Last non-zero remainder = HCF
HCF(270, 192):
270 = 1×192 + 78 → HCF(192, 78)
192 = 2×78 + 36 → HCF(78, 36)
78 = 2×36 + 6 → HCF(36, 6)
36 = 6×6 + 0 → HCF = 6
⚡ CDS Shortcut: HCF by Difference Method

Fast HCF Trick

  • HCF of numbers = HCF of their differences
  • HCF(60, 84) = HCF(84-60, 60) = HCF(24, 60) = HCF(24, 36) = HCF(24, 12) = 12
  • Or: Try common small primes (2, 3, 5, 7) first — fastest for MCQs
HCF of consecutive integersAlways = 1 (they are co-prime)
HCF of consecutive evenHCF(2n, 2n+2) = 2
HCF(n, n+1)Always = 1
HCF ≤ smaller numberHCF is always ≤ min(a,b)
HCF divides LCMLCM is always divisible by HCF
HCF(a, 0) = aAny number and 0 → HCF = that number

LCM — Lowest Common Multiple

3 methods · Applications · Clock/Bells problems

♾️
LCM — Complete Methods & ApplicationsHIGH YIELD
▶
LCM = Smallest number divisible by all given numbers
Lowest Common Multiple · Used in bells, cycles, time-work problems
Method 1: Prime Factorization for LCM
RULE

Take HIGHEST power of ALL prime factors (common AND uncommon)

LCM(12, 18, 24):
12 = 2² × 3 | 18 = 2 × 3² | 24 = 2³ × 3
LCM = 2³ × 3² = 8 × 9 = 72
Method 2: Division (Ladder) Method — Fastest for MCQs
LCM(12, 15, 20) — Division Ladder

⚡ LCM Shortcut Tips

  • LCM ≥ max(a, b) always
  • If one number divides another: LCM = larger number
  • LCM of co-primes = their product
  • LCM(n, 1) = n always
  • For fractions: LCM = LCM(numerators) / HCF(denominators)

🔔 Bell/Clock Application

Bells ring at intervals of a, b, c seconds. They ring together at: LCM(a, b, c) seconds.

Bells at 5,7,10 min intervals:
LCM(5,7,10) = 70 minutes
Ring together every 70 min

⚠️ LCM Traps in CDS/NDA

  • For fractions: LCM of 2/3 and 3/4 → LCM = LCM(2,3)/HCF(3,4) = 6/1 = 6 (NOT 6/12)
  • LCM of fractions uses LCM of numerators / HCF of denominators
  • HCF of fractions uses HCF of numerators / LCM of denominators

HCF × LCM Relation

The golden formula · Applications · PYQ patterns

🔗
The Golden Relationship — Most Tested Formula⭐ STAR TOPIC
▶
HCF(a,b) × LCM(a,b) = a × b
Product of two numbers = HCF × LCM
If a = 12, b = 18: HCF = 6, LCM = 36
Check: 6 × 36 = 216 = 12 × 18 ✓
LCM = (a × b) / HCF
Rearranged form — find LCM if HCF known
Also: HCF = (a × b) / LCM
Also: Second number = (HCF × LCM) / First number

⚠️ CRITICAL: When Does the Formula NOT Apply?

  • Formula works ONLY for TWO numbers
  • For THREE or more numbers: Product ≠ HCF × LCM
  • For three numbers: a×b×c = HCF(a,b,c) × LCM(a,b,c) × [additional factor] — NOT a simple formula
  • CDS loves to ask: "HCF=6, LCM=72, one number=18, find other" → Use: other = (6×72)/18 = 24
Given HCF + one numberOther = (HCF × LCM) ÷ First
HCF divides bothIf HCF(a,b)=h, then a=hm, b=hn where HCF(m,n)=1
LCM divisible by HCFAlways: LCM = HCF × integer
Quick checkLCM/HCF must always be integer

Advanced HCF & LCM Concepts

Co-primes · Fractions · Special cases

⚡
Co-primes, Fraction HCF/LCM & Special CasesADVANCED
▶

🔵 Co-prime Numbers

  • HCF(a,b) = 1 → a,b are co-prime
  • No common factor except 1
  • Examples: (8,9), (14,15), (35,36)
  • Consecutive integers are always co-prime
  • LCM of co-primes = their product

📐 HCF of Fractions

HCF(fractions) = HCF(numerators) / LCM(denominators)
HCF(2/3, 4/9, 6/27)
= HCF(2,4,6) / LCM(3,9,27)
= 2 / 27

📐 LCM of Fractions

LCM(fractions) = LCM(numerators) / HCF(denominators)
LCM(2/3, 4/9, 6/27)
= LCM(2,4,6) / HCF(3,9,27)
= 12 / 3 = 4

🧠 Memory Hook: HCF/LCM of Fractions

"HCF = H over L · LCM = L over H"

HCF of fractions: HCF numerators over LCM denominators
LCM of fractions: LCM numerators over HCF denominators

Decimals — Introduction

Place value · Decimal fractions · Types

🔹
Decimal System — Place Value & TypesFOUNDATION
▶
DECIMAL PLACE VALUE CHART

✅ Terminating Decimals

  • Decimal expansion ends after finite digits
  • Example: 3/4 = 0.75
  • When: denominator has only 2s and/or 5s
  • 1/8 = 0.125, 3/25 = 0.12

🔄 Recurring/Non-terminating

  • Digits repeat infinitely
  • 1/3 = 0.3333… = 0.3̄
  • 1/7 = 0.142857142857… = 0.1̄42857̄
  • When denominator has primes other than 2 & 5

🔢 Key Termination Rule

p/q (reduced form) terminates ⟺ q = 2^a × 5^b

  • 1/4 = 1/2² → terminates
  • 1/6 = 1/(2×3) → recurring
  • 7/40 = 7/(2³×5) → terminates

Decimal Operations

Add · Subtract · Multiply · Divide · Shortcuts

➕
Decimal Operations — Rules & ShortcutsSPEED MATH
▶

➕ Addition & Subtraction

  • Align decimal points before computing
  • Add trailing zeros if needed to equalize lengths
  • 3.45 + 12.6 = 3.45 + 12.60 = 16.05
  • Carry over works same as integers

✖️ Multiplication

  • Multiply ignoring decimal points
  • Count total decimal places in both numbers
  • Place decimal in result from right
  • 2.4 × 3.5: 24×35=840, 2 decimal places → 8.40

➗ Division

  • Make divisor a whole number by multiplying both by 10ⁿ
  • 3.6 ÷ 0.4 = 36 ÷ 4 = 9
  • Or move decimal right in both: same number of places
  • 0.072 ÷ 0.09 = 72 ÷ 90 = 0.8

⚡ Decimal Shift Trick

  • ×10 → shift decimal right by 1
  • ×100 → shift right by 2
  • ÷10 → shift decimal left by 1
  • 0.0056 × 1000 = 5.6
  • 345 ÷ 1000 = 0.345

⚡ CDS Speed Trick: Decimal Multiplication

1.25 × 48 = 5/4 × 48 = 5 × 12 = 60 (convert decimal to fraction when easy!)
0.125 = 1/8; 0.25 = 1/4; 0.375 = 3/8; 0.5 = 1/2; 0.75 = 3/4; 0.875 = 7/8

  • Digit ≥ 5: round up | Digit < 5: round down
  • Round 3.456 to 2 decimal places: 3.46
  • Round 7.835 to 1 decimal place: 7.8 (if 5, round to even)

  • ÷0.5 = ×2 → 36 ÷ 0.5 = 72

Use HCF when: "maximum", "largest", "biggest piece", "divide equally", "measure exactly"

  • For MCQs: check if answer options divide all given numbers

  • HCF(a,b) divides (a-b)

Those numbers = k×D + r

Common CDS/UPSC Traps & Pitfalls

Most frequent mistakes · PYQ error analysis

⚠️
Top Exam Traps — Don't Lose These Marks!CRITICAL
▶

Trap 1: HCF × LCM for 3+ Numbers

  • a×b = HCF(a,b) × LCM(a,b) — ONLY for 2 numbers!
  • For 3 numbers: a×b×c ≠ HCF × LCM (without correction factor)
  • Use prime factorization method for 3+ numbers

Trap 2: HCF/LCM of Fractions

  • HCF of fractions ≠ HCF of numerators alone
  • Must use: HCF(p/q) = HCF(p) / LCM(q)
  • LCM of fractions ≠ LCM of numerators alone
  • Must use: LCM(p/q) = LCM(p) / HCF(q)

Trap 3: Recurring Decimal Conversion

  • 0.9̄ = 9/9 = 1 (exactly equal to 1!)
  • 0.3̄ = 1/3 (exactly, not approximately)
  • For mixed: 0.12̄ → numerator = 12-1 = 11, denominator = 90
  • Count non-recurring digits for denominator zeros

Trap 4: 1 and Prime Numbers

  • 1 is NEITHER prime NOR composite — always tested!
  • 2 is the ONLY even prime number
  • All other primes are odd
  • Prime numbers have exactly 2 factors (1 and itself)

Trap 5: Decimal Place Count in Multiplication

  • 2.3 × 0.03 = 0.069 (not 0.69!)
  • Count ALL decimal places in both numbers
  • 2.3 has 1, 0.03 has 2 → total 3 decimal places
  • Product of 23×3 = 69 → 0.069

Trap 6: Remainder Problems

  • "Leaves same remainder" → find HCF of DIFFERENCES of numbers
  • "Divisible" often means look for LCM
  • Smallest number divisible by a,b,c = LCM(a,b,c)
  • Greatest number dividing a,b,c = HCF(a,b,c)

🔢 HCF = LOWEST, LCM = HIGHEST

"HCF Hunts Low, LCM Leaps High"

HCF uses LOWEST powers of common factors.
LCM uses HIGHEST powers of ALL factors.

🔄 Fractions Rule Memory

"HCF = High/Low | LCM = Low/High"

HCF of fractions: HCF(num) / LCM(den) — Top-heavy fraction

📊 Divisibility by 11 — Chess Rule

"Odd Minus Even = 0 or 11"

Sum of digits at ODD positions − sum at EVEN positions = multiple of 11 (or 0)
5489: (5+8) − (4+9) = 13-13 = 0 ✓ divisible by 11!

🔁 Recurring to Fraction Rule

"9s for pure recurring, Mix with 0s"

Pure recurring (all digits repeat): Denominator = same number of 9s
Mixed (some non-recurring): 9s = recurring count, 0s = non-recurring count
0.1̄2̄3̄ = 123/999 | 0.12̄3̄ = (123-1)/990 = 122/990

Find the HCF of 144, 180, and 108.

Answer: 36

Q2
The LCM of two numbers is 48 and their HCF is 4. If one number is 12, find the other.

Answer: 16

Other number = (HCF × LCM) / First number
= (4 × 48) / 12 = 192 / 12 = 16

Analytical · Application · Reasoning-based

C1
Three runners A, B, C complete one round of a circular track in 4, 6, and 9 minutes respectively. All start together. After how many minutes will they all be at the starting point simultaneously?

Answer: 36 minutes

LCM(4, 6, 9):
4 = 2² | 6 = 2×3 | 9 = 3²
LCM = 2² × 3² = 4 × 9 = 36 minutes
All three meet at starting point after 36 minutes.

CSAT Application: Circular track problems always use LCM. When they ask "when at same point," it's LCM of time periods. When they ask "when does the faster lap the slower," find LCM and subtract starting times.

C2
A number when divided by 6, 7, 8, 9, and 10 leaves a remainder of 1 in each case. What is the smallest such number?

Answer: 2521

Required number = LCM(6,7,8,9,10) + 1
LCM(6,7,8,9,10):
6=2×3 | 7=7 | 8=2³ | 9=3² | 10=2×5
LCM = 2³ × 3² × 5 × 7 = 8 × 9 × 5 × 7 = 2520
Required number = 2520 + 1 = 2521

CSAT Insight: "Leaves remainder r from all divisors" → LCM + r. This is one of the most important CSAT patterns.

C3
What is the greatest number which when divided into 340 and 445 leaves the same remainder?

Answer: 5 (or any factor of 105)

When a number N divides both 340 and 445 with same remainder,
N must divide their difference: 445 - 340 = 105
Factors of 105 = 1, 3, 5, 7, 15, 21, 35, 105
Greatest = 105... but usually "greatest" without remainder specification means 105.
Verify: 340 = 3×105 + 25; 445 = 4×105 + 25 ✓ (same remainder 25)

Key concept: Same remainder → difference is divisible by the divisor → find HCF of differences.

C4
Two tankers of 750 litres and 1125 litres need to be exactly filled using same size container. What is the largest container size possible?

Answer: 375 litres

Largest container = HCF(750, 1125)
750 = 2 × 3 × 5³
1125 = 3² × 5³
HCF = 3 × 5³ = 3 × 125 = 375 litres
Check: 750÷375=2 ✓, 1125÷375=3 ✓

Application type: "Exact fill" or "measure exactly" = HCF problem. Largest possible = HCF.

C5
If HCF of two numbers is 11 and their LCM is 693, find how many such pairs of numbers are possible.

Answer: 2 pairs

Both numbers = 11a and 11b where HCF(a,b)=1
LCM = 11ab = 693 → ab = 63
Pairs of co-prime numbers with product 63:
63 = 7 × 9 → HCF(7,9)=1 ✓ → (77, 99)
63 = 1 × 63 → HCF(1,63)=1 ✓ → (11, 693)
63 = 3 × 21 → HCF(3,21)=3 ✗
63 = 9 × 7 (same as first) → 2 distinct pairs

Advanced HCF concept: Express as 11a and 11b, find co-prime pairs with product = LCM/HCF.

C6
A person has three pieces of string of length 66 cm, 110 cm, and 198 cm. He wants to cut them into equal pieces without any wastage. What is the maximum number of pieces he can get?

Answer: 21 pieces

Largest piece size = HCF(66, 110, 198)
66 = 2 × 3 × 11
110 = 2 × 5 × 11
198 = 2 × 3² × 11
HCF = 2 × 11 = 22 cm
Pieces from each: 66/22=3, 110/22=5, 198/22=9
Total pieces = 3 + 5 + 9 = 17 pieces

Total pieces = sum of (each length / HCF). HCF gives maximum piece size; sum of quotients = total count.

C7
Prove that 0.9̄ = 1 exactly. Why is this mathematically true?

Answer: 0.9̄ = 1 (exactly, not approximately)

Method 1 (Algebraic):
Let x = 0.9̄ = 0.9999...
10x = 9.9999... = 9 + 0.9999...
10x = 9 + x
9x = 9 → x = 1

Method 2 (Fraction):
0.9̄ = 9/9 = 1

Method 3 (Sum of series):
0.9+0.09+0.009+... = 0.9/(1-0.1) = 0.9/0.9 = 1

CSAT Concept: This is a common trick question. 0.9̄ is identically equal to 1 — not approximately. The real number system has no "infinitesimally small" positive gap between them.

C8
Find the smallest number which when increased by 7 is exactly divisible by 15, 20, 24, and 32.

Answer: 473

Required: number + 7 = LCM(15, 20, 24, 32)
LCM(15,20,24,32):
15=3×5 | 20=2²×5 | 24=2³×3 | 32=2⁵
LCM = 2⁵ × 3 × 5 = 32 × 15 = 480
Required number = 480 - 7 = 473

Pattern: "When increased by k, divisible by..." → Required = LCM - k. "When decreased by k" → LCM + k.

C10
A decimal number D = 0.142857142857... What fraction does D represent? What pattern do you notice about 1/7?

Answer: D = 1/7; The pattern 142857 is a cyclic number

D = 0.142857̄ = 142857/999999 = 1/7
The beauty: 1/7 = 0.142857..., 2/7 = 0.285714..., 3/7 = 0.428571...
Same 6 digits cycled! 142857 is a cyclic number.
142857 × 1 = 142857
142857 × 2 = 285714 (cycled!)
142857 × 7 = 999999

CSAT insight: 1/7 has period 6. Any fraction p/q with q prime has period dividing (q-1) (Fermat's little theorem). 1/7: period = 6 = 7-1. 1/11: period = 2. 1/13: period = 6.

C11
Can two numbers have HCF = 45 and LCM = 100? Justify.

Answer: NO — impossible

For two numbers, LCM must be divisible by HCF.
Check: 100 ÷ 45 = 2.22... (not an integer!)
Since LCM must be a multiple of HCF (both numbers are multiples of HCF, so LCM must be too),
and 100 is NOT divisible by 45,
such a pair CANNOT exist.

Key concept: LCM is ALWAYS a multiple of HCF. If LCM/HCF is not an integer → impossible combination. Standard CSAT "is it possible?" question type.

C13
A number is simultaneously divisible by 4, 5, and 6. It leaves a remainder of 3 when divided by 7. Find the smallest such number.

Answer: 360

Divisible by 4, 5, 6 → must be multiple of LCM(4,5,6)
LCM(4,5,6) = 60
Multiples of 60: 60, 120, 180, 240, 300, 360, 420...
Check remainder when divided by 7:
60 ÷ 7 = 8 rem 4 ✗
120 ÷ 7 = 17 rem 1 ✗
180 ÷ 7 = 25 rem 5 ✗
240 ÷ 7 = 34 rem 2 ✗
300 ÷ 7 = 42 rem 6 ✗
360 ÷ 7 = 51 rem 3 ✓ → Answer: 360
C14
Data Interpretation: A survey of 240 people found 0.375 prefer tea, 5/12 prefer coffee, and the rest prefer juice. How many prefer juice?

Answer: 50 people prefer juice

Tea: 0.375 × 240 = 3/8 × 240 = 90 people
Coffee: 5/12 × 240 = 100 people
Total tea + coffee = 90 + 100 = 190
Juice = 240 - 190 = 50 people

Decimal to fraction conversion speed matters here: 0.375 = 3/8 (memory: 375×8=3000). Converting before multiplying is faster than decimal arithmetic.

C15
Statement 1: The LCM of two prime numbers p and q (p≠q) is always p×q. Statement 2: The HCF of two consecutive natural numbers is always 1. Which statements are correct?

Answer: Both Statement 1 and Statement 2 are correct

Statement 1: Two distinct primes share no common factors except 1 → HCF=1 → they are co-prime → LCM = p×q ✓
Example: LCM(7,11) = 77 = 7×11 ✓

Statement 2: Consecutive numbers n and n+1:
Any common factor d divides both n and n+1
→ d divides (n+1)-n = 1
→ d = 1 → HCF(n, n+1) = 1 always ✓
Both statements are TRUE.

Classic CSAT assertion-reason: Two distinct primes are always co-prime. Consecutive integers are always co-prime (difference = 1, which only has factor 1).
